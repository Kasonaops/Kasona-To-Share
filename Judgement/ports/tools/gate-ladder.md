# Tool: Gate ladder

## What it is

A fixed order of questions where each one is a gate: whoever fails question two never gets
question four. The order is the content. It is a generalisation of how one disciplined investor
sequences questions before committing capital, stripped of anything specific to investing. The
generic shape is: understand it, is it a good kind of thing, is it in good hands, is the price of
entry sane, does the expected return justify the time, and what do you lose if you are wrong.

## Reach for it when

You are choosing whether to enter something: a client, a partnership, a job, a venture, a
supplier, a deal. And you notice you are running several checks in parallel, none of which is
allowed to end the conversation. The ladder lets the cheap ones end it.

## Steps

Adapt the wording to your field (the domain pack may help). Answer each gate with a reason, and
stop at the first failed gate.

| Gate | Question | Passing means |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Understand | Can I explain what this is and how it works, in two plain sentences, without jargon? | Yes. If not, stop and learn, or stop |
| 2. Kind | Is this the kind of thing that tends to hold up: a durable advantage, a structural reason it should keep working? | A named reason, not a hope |
| 3. Hands | Is it run by people whose incentives match mine? | Evidence of how they behave when it costs them |
| 4. Entry price | Is the cost of entry sane against what I can expect back? | Stated as a range, not one number |
| 5. Return | Does the expected result justify my time, over the whole time it takes? | A rough rate of return on time, with the basis stated |
| 6. Downside | If I am wrong, what exactly do I lose, and can I survive it? | The loss stated in concrete terms, sized against what I can afford to lose |

Then ask the question that turns "looks good" into "is good":

> **Why is this on offer to me, and why do I think I am right where others disagree?**

Name the specific thing others are misreading. "It is cheap" or "it is available" is an
observation, not an argument. If the honest answer is "I do not know", the thesis is not
finished, however well gates 1 to 6 went. Write that answer down as an explicit assumption, so
the review can check it: either the others' misreading got corrected, or they were right and a
real problem was read as a temporary one.

A useful side question at gate 6: is this a **temporary problem** or a **fundamental
deterioration**? Both look identical from the outside. Only the first makes the low entry price
an opportunity.

## What a bad application looks like

- Running all six gates but writing answers so vague that nothing can fail. A gate that cannot
  fail is not a gate.
- Skipping gate 6 because 1 to 5 felt good. The downside gate comes last and is still the gate.
- Treating gates 4 and 5 as one question. Cheap to enter is a statement about today. Return is a
  statement about what happens over the time it takes. Something can pass the first and fail the
  second.
- Letting the "why is this on offer to me" question resolve to "because I am smart".

## Where its output lands in the Decision Record

**Screen result** (which gate it stopped at, or that it cleared them all), **Consequences** and
**Probabilities** (gates 5 and 6), and the "why on offer" answer as an explicit assumption in
**Revisit conditions**.
