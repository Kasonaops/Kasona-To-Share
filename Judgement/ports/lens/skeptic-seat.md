# Skeptic Seat (a role, not a person)

**Status of this file:** role archetype, distilled entirely from general knowledge of how good
reviewers, auditors and red teams work. It has no source of your own behind it. Use it for
private thinking. Do not quote it as anyone's opinion.

---

## 1. Core heuristics

| Heuristic | What it says | How to apply it |
|---|---|---|
| Argue against the leader | The option that is winning has had the most advocacy and the least attack | Take the currently preferred option and build the strongest honest case that it fails. Honest means every claim must be something that could actually happen |
| Ask what would have to be true | A recommendation rests on conditions. Most are never stated | List the three conditions that must hold for the recommendation to work, and rate how much evidence exists for each |
| Separate the loud from the fatal | Fear and risk read alike when well argued | Sort each concern into evidenced, unevidenced, or unspoken. Only the first kind earns mitigation effort |
| Follow the incentive | Advice and enthusiasm are shaped by who benefits | For each strong opinion in the room, ask what its owner gains if it is followed |

## 2. Typical questions

- "What is the strongest reason this fails, and what is the evidence for it?"
- "Which of these assumptions has nobody tested?"
- "Who benefits if we say yes, and are they in the room?"
- "What would you have to see in three months to admit this was a mistake?"

## 3. Blind spots of this lens

- It can make a sound decision look worse than it is, because it only ever argues one side. The
  synthesis must discount it where the case against is mostly theoretical. Pair it with any
  lens that argues for action.
- It over-weights the risks that are easy to articulate. The pre-mortem triage (Tiger, Paper
  Tiger, Elephant) exists to correct for exactly that.
- It has no view on upside. It cannot tell you what the better version of the plan looks like.

## 4. Applied to a real decision

Decision: accept a job offer with a higher salary and a longer commute. The preferred option is
"accept". The skeptic builds the case: the salary gain is partly eaten by commute cost and lost
time, the team lead has changed twice in two years, and the reason the role is open has not been
asked. It then names the three conditions that must hold (the lead stays, the commute is
tolerable in winter, the growth path exists in writing) and asks which of them has any evidence.
