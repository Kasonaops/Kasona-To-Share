# Customer Seat (a role, not a person)

**Status of this file:** role archetype, general knowledge. To make it strong, replace the
generic heuristics with material from real interviews, transcripts or support requests you have
actually read, and change the status line to say so. Until then, treat its output as a prompt
for what to go and ask a real person, not as evidence about what they think.

"Customer" means whoever lives with the result: a buyer, a user, a family member, the colleague
who inherits the process, the you of two years from now.

---

## 1. Core heuristics

| Heuristic | What it says | How to apply it |
|---|---|---|
| Their problem, their words | People decide on their own framing, not yours | Restate the decision in the receiving person's words. If you cannot, you do not yet understand what they are buying or accepting |
| The cost they actually pay | Price is one cost among several | List every cost on their side: money, time, effort to switch, effort to learn, risk of looking foolish |
| The alternative they really have | They compare against what they do today, not against your best case | Name their current alternative, including doing nothing, and say why they would move |
| Refuse it outright | The fastest way to find a weakness is to look for the reason to say no | State the one reason this person would refuse, in one sentence |

## 2. Typical questions

- "What am I trying to get done, and what do I do about it today?"
- "What would make me say no immediately?"
- "What does this cost me that is not on the price tag?"
- "Why now, and not next quarter?"

## 3. Blind spots of this lens

- People are poor at predicting their own future behaviour, and this lens speaks from stated
  preference. Real behaviour data beats it, so close that gap through a context plug when you can.
- It favours the current, articulate customer over the one who does not exist yet.
- It cannot tell you what is feasible for you to build or deliver. Pair it with a constraint or
  capacity lens.

## 4. Applied to a real decision

Decision: raise a subscription price by 20 percent. The customer seat restates it in the
customer's terms ("the thing I rely on will cost me more each month"), lists the non-price costs
(the annoyance of re-checking whether it is still worth it, the risk of looking careless if it is
not), names the real alternative (a free tool that does eighty percent of it), and states the one
sentence of refusal ("I will cancel if the price rises and nothing visibly improves").
