# Temporal Triangulation

Look at the same problem from three time perspectives (past, present, future) to surface
patterns, blind spots, and opportunities that a single-perspective analysis misses.

## Example scenario

"How did organizations preserve expert knowledge in 1970? How do we do it today? How will it
work in 2045? What piece of that future solution could we already prototype today?"

## Description

Analyze a problem or topic from three distinct time perspectives to identify fundamental
patterns and derive solutions that hold up over time, not just in the current moment.

## Input

- **The problem or topic** (required).
- **Specific time points for the analysis** (optional, e.g. 1970, 2025, 2045; otherwise pick
  eras that fit the question).

## Output

A structured three-era analysis:

- **Past analysis**: methods, constraints, and principles at play.
- **Present analysis**: current assumptions and best practices.
- **Future analysis**: expected developments and future standards.
- **Pattern analysis**: constants, paradigm shifts, and blind spots across all three eras.
- **Recommendation**: a future-shaped solution you could act on today.

## Context

This technique helps break out of a present-tense thinking rut. "Modern" solutions are often
just a variant of an old approach, and "futuristic" ideas are frequently already implementable
today. The most valuable insight usually sits in what stays constant across every era, not in
what changes.

## Process

### Step 1: Analyze the past

- How was this problem solved in the chosen era?
- What technologies, methods, or principles were used?
- What were the constraints and the available options?

### Step 2: Analyze the present

- How do we solve it today?
- What has changed since the past era?
- What are our current assumptions and best practices?

### Step 3: Project the future

- How will it likely be solved?
- What technologies will be standard?
- What will feel obvious in the future that looks impossible today?

### Step 4: Find the patterns

- What stays constant across all three eras? These are the fundamental principles.
- What paradigm shifts happened between eras?
- What are we doing today that will look absurd in the future?
- What "future solution" is already possible today, but blocked by outdated thinking?

### Step 5: Derive a recommendation

Which piece of the future-shaped approach can be prototyped today?

### Rules

- Be specific within each era, not generic.
- Focus on actionable insight, not just observation.
- The recommendation must be concrete and something you could actually start.

### Avoid

- Science-fiction speculation with no logical basis.
- Stopping at analysis without a recommendation.

## Definition of done

- [ ] All three eras are analyzed specifically, not generically.
- [ ] At least two constants identified across all eras.
- [ ] At least one paradigm shift identified.
- [ ] At least one "future solution for today" derived.
- [ ] The recommendation is concrete and actionable.

## Applying this to an investment thesis

A common use inside a portfolio review: a thesis that's gone stale (roughly a year or more
since it was last actively challenged) is a good candidate for this exact structure, applied
to the investment case itself rather than to a general problem:

- **Past**: what was the original thesis when the position was opened? What assumptions did
  it depend on, and what has since been proven right or wrong?
- **Present**: what does the business actually look like today? Which parts of the original
  thesis are still standing on their original logic, and which are being defended by newer,
  different reasoning that quietly replaced the original one?
- **Future**: given where the business and its industry are heading, does the original thesis
  still describe where this is going, or has the real reason to hold shifted into something
  that was never written down?

The constant worth looking for here is specific: is there a piece of the original thesis that
has held up across every period, and is that constant actually still true, or has it just
never been re-examined? The recommendation this produces is usually one of: reaffirm the
thesis as originally written, rewrite it to reflect what's actually true now, or treat the
staleness itself as a signal to route the position into a full exit-case review.
